    Section 1.18 Cryptology

    Subsection 1.18.1 What is cryptography?

    ‘Crypto’ comes from the Greek \(\kappa\rho\upsilon\pi\tau\omega\zeta\\text{,}\) which means “hidden” or “concealed”. ‘Graph’ comes from \(\gamma\rho\alpha\phi\omega\text{,}\) meaning “to write”.
    🔗

    Definition 1.18.1. Cryptology.

    Cryptography is the design of systems allowing for secure communication over a nonsecure channel.
    🔗
    Cryptology is the overall study or science of secure communication.
    🔗
    Cryptanalysis is the practice or science of breaking cryptographic systems.
    🔗
    A cryptographic system takes a message \(m\text{,}\) the plaintext, encrypts it to a ciphertext, \(c\text{,}\) whose meaning can only be fathomed by decrypting back to the plaintext \(m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.18.3. Jargon disentanglement.

    Cryptography is to be distinguished from coding theory which deals more with the representation of data and its communication over noisy channels. It should also be distinguished from steganography (’stegano’ means “covered”), which is the practice of physically hiding messages (with invisible ink, under wax coatings, on the inside of egg shells, etc.).
    🔗
    Cryptography doesn’t hide the message itself, but rather the meaning of the message.
    🔗
    🔗

    The Caesar shift cipher is a cryptosystem allegedly used by Julius Caesar which encrpyts a plaintext \(m\) by replacing each letter with the letter three steps away (in a cyclic manner).

    Definition 1.18.6. Cryptosystem.

    A cryptosystem consists of a set of keys \(\mathcal{K}\text{,}\) called the keyspace, sets \(\mathcal{P}\) and \(\mathcal{C}\) consisting of the possible plaintexts and ciphertexts, respectively, and for each key \(k\in \mathcal{K}\) a pair of functions
    \begin{align*} E_k\colon \mathcal{P} \amp \rightarrow \mathcal{C}\\ D_k\colon \mathcal{C} \amp \rightarrow \mathcal{P} \end{align*}
    satisfying \(D_k(E_k(p))=p\) for all \(p\in \mathcal{P}\text{.}\) For each \(k\in \mathcal{K}\text{,}\) the functions \(E_k\) and \(D_k\) are called the encryption and decryption functions for the key \(k\).
    🔗
    Equivalently, we can describe a cryptosystem as a pair of functions
    \begin{align} E\colon \mathcal{K}\times \mathcal{P}\amp \rightarrow \mathcal{C} \amp D\colon \mathcal{K}\times \mathcal{C} \amp\rightarrow \mathcal{P}\tag{1.18.1}\\ (k,p) \amp \rightarrow E_k(p) \amp (k,c)\amp\mapsto D_k(c)\tag{1.18.2} \end{align}
    such that for all \(k\in \mathcal{K}\) and all \(p\in \mathcal{P}\text{,}\) \(D(k,E(k,p))=p\text{.}\) The functions \(E\) and \(D\) are called the encryption and decryption methods of the cryptosystem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Mantra 1.18.7. Kerchoff’s principle.

    The security of a cryptosystem \((E,D)\) should not rely on the methods \(E\) and \(D\) being kept secret.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.18.2 Subsititution cipher

    Definition 1.18.8. Symmetric group.

    Let \(X=\{x_1,x_2,\dots, x_n\}\) be a finite group of cardinality \(n\text{.}\) A permutation of \(X\) is a bijection (equivalently, invertible function) \(\sigma\colon X\rightarrow X\text{.}\) The set \(S_X\) of all permutations of \(X\) is called the symmetric group on \(X\text{.}\) In the case where \(X=\{1,2,\dots, n\}\text{,}\) we write \(S_n\) for \(S_X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.18.9. Subsitution cipher.

    Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be a set alphabet. The substitution cipher on \(\mathcal{A}\) is the cryptosystem with \(\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\text{,}\) keyspace \(\mathcal{K}=S_{\mathcal{A}}\) and encryption and decryption methods \(E\) and \(D\) defined as
    \begin{align*} E_\sigma(p) \amp =\sigma(p) \amp D_\sigma(c)=\sigma^{-1}(c) \end{align*}
    for all \(\sigma\in S_\mathcal{A}\text{,}\) \(p\in \mathcal{P}=\mathcal{A}\) and \(c\in\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.18.3 Vigenère cipher

    What made the substitution cipher vulnerable to frequency analysis is that the encryption was defined on the level of single letters. We call this a monographic cryptosystem. A polygraphic (block) cryptosystem, in contrast, defines an encrpytion method on a block, or list of letters. The Vignère cipher defined below is a classic example of a block cipher that at first glance appears to be invulnerable to frequency analysis, and indeed was long considered to be unbreakable.
    🔗

    Definition 1.18.11. Vigenère cipher.

    Let \(\mathcal{A}=\{0,1,\dots, 25\}\text{,}\) and let \(n\) be a positive integer. Define
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{K}=\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}^n\text{,} \end{equation*}
    and encryption methods \(E\) and \(D\) as
    \begin{align*} E_{(k_1,k_2,\dots, k_n)}(p_1,p_2,\dots, p_n) \amp = (p_1+k_1\boldmod{26}, p_2+k_2\boldmod{26},\dots p_n+k_n\boldmod{26})\\ D_{(k_1,k_2,\dots, k_n)}(c_1,c_2,\dots, c_n) \amp = (c_1-k_1\boldmod{26}, c_2-k_2\boldmod{26},\dots c_n-k_n\boldmod{26}) \end{align*}
    for each key \(k=(k_1,k_2,\dots, k_n)\in \mathcal{K}\text{,}\) plaintext \(p=(p_1,p_2,\dots, p_n)\in \mathcal{P}\) and ciphertext \(c=(c_1,c_2,\dots, c_n)\in \mathcal{C}\text{.}\)

    🔗
