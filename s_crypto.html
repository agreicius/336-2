    Section 1.18 Cryptography

    Subsection 1.18.1 What is cryptography?

    ‘Crypto’ comes from the Greek \(\kappa\rho\upsilon\pi\tau\omega\zeta\\text{,}\) which means “hidden” or “concealed”. ‘Graph’ comes from \(\gamma\rho\alpha\phi\omega\text{,}\) meaning “to write”.
    🔗

    Definition 1.18.1. Cryptography.

    Cryptography is the design of systems allowing for secure communication over a nonsecure channel.
    🔗
    Cryptology is the overall study or science of secure communication.
    🔗
    Cryptanalysis is the practice or science of breaking cryptographic systems.
    🔗
    A cryptographic system takes a message \(m\text{,}\) the plaintext, encrypts it to a ciphertext, \(c\text{,}\) whose meaning can only be fathomed by decrypting back to the plaintext \(m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.18.3. Jargon disentanglement.

    Cryptography is to be distinguished from coding theory which deals more with the representation of data and its communication over noisy channels. It should also be distinguished from steganography (’stegano’ means “covered”), which is the practice of physically hiding messages (with invisible ink, under wax coatings, on the inside of egg shells, etc.).
    🔗
    Cryptography doesn’t hide the message itself, but rather the meaning of the message.
    🔗
    🔗

    The Caesar shift cipher is a cryptosystem allegedly used by Julius Caesar which encrpyts a plaintext \(m\) by replacing each letter with the letter three steps away (in a cyclic manner).

    Definition 1.18.6. Cryptosystem.

    A cryptosystem consists of a set of keys \(\mathcal{K}\text{,}\) called the keyspace, sets \(\mathcal{P}\) and \(\mathcal{C}\) consisting of the possible plaintexts and ciphertexts, respectively, and for each key \(k\in \mathcal{K}\) a pair of functions
    \begin{align*} E_k\colon \mathcal{P} \amp \rightarrow \mathcal{C}\\ D_k\colon \mathcal{C} \amp \rightarrow \mathcal{P} \end{align*}
    satisfying \(D_k(E_k(p))=p\) for all \(p\in \mathcal{P}\text{.}\) For each \(k\in \mathcal{K}\text{,}\) the functions \(E_k\) and \(D_k\) are called the encryption and decryption functions for the key \(k\).
    🔗
    Equivalently, we can describe a cryptosystem as a pair of functions
    \begin{align} E\colon \mathcal{K}\times \mathcal{P}\amp \rightarrow \mathcal{C} \amp D\colon \mathcal{K}\times \mathcal{C} \amp\rightarrow \mathcal{P}\tag{1.18.1}\\ (k,p) \amp \rightarrow E_k(p) \amp (k,c)\amp\mapsto D_k(c)\tag{1.18.2} \end{align}
    such that for all \(k\in \mathcal{K}\) and all \(p\in \mathcal{P}\text{,}\) \(D(k,E(k,p))=p\text{.}\) The functions \(E\) and \(D\) are called the encryption and decryption methods of the cryptosystem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Mantra 1.18.7. Kerchoff’s principle.

    The security of a cryptosystem \((E,D)\) should not rely on the methods \(E\) and \(D\) being kept secret.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.18.2 Subsititution cipher

    Definition 1.18.8. Symmetric group.

    Let \(X=\{x_1,x_2,\dots, x_n\}\) be a finite group of cardinality \(n\text{.}\) A permutation of \(X\) is a bijection (equivalently, invertible function) \(\sigma\colon X\rightarrow X\text{.}\) The set \(S_X\) of all permutations of \(X\) is called the symmetric group on \(X\text{.}\) In the case where \(X=\{1,2,\dots, n\}\text{,}\) we write \(S_n\) for \(S_X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.18.9. Subsitution cipher.

    Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be a set alphabet. The substitution cipher on \(\mathcal{A}\) is the cryptosystem with \(\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\text{,}\) keyspace \(\mathcal{K}=S_{\mathcal{A}}\) and encryption and decryption methods \(E\) and \(D\) defined as
    \begin{align*} E_\sigma(p) \amp =\sigma(p) \amp D_\sigma(c)=\sigma^{-1}(c) \end{align*}
    for all \(\sigma\in S_\mathcal{A}\text{,}\) \(p\in \mathcal{P}=\mathcal{A}\) and \(c\in\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.18.3 Vigenère cipher

    What made the substitution cipher vulnerable to frequency analysis is that the encryption was defined on the level of single letters. We call this a monographic cryptosystem. A polygraphic (block) cryptosystem, in contrast, defines an encryption method on a block, or list of letters. The Vignère cipher defined below is a classic example of a block cipher that at first glance appears to be invulnerable to frequency analysis, and indeed was long considered to be unbreakable. The Vignère cipher was popularized by the French diplomat Blaise de Vignère in the 16th century, when during a trip to Rome he became familiar with some of the cryptographic techniques of Leon Alberti, a 15th century Italian scholar and quintessential Renaissance man (poet, composer, painter, etc.).
    🔗

    Definition 1.18.11. Vigenère cipher.

    Let \(\mathcal{A}=\{0,1,\dots, 25\}\text{,}\) and let \(n\) be a positive integer. Define
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{K}=\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}^n\text{,} \end{equation*}
    and encryption methods \(E\) and \(D\) as
    \begin{align*} E_{(k_1,k_2,\dots, k_n)}(p_1,p_2,\dots, p_n) \amp = (p_1+k_1\boldmod{26}, p_2+k_2\boldmod{26},\dots p_n+k_n\boldmod{26})\\ D_{(k_1,k_2,\dots, k_n)}(c_1,c_2,\dots, c_n) \amp = (c_1-k_1\boldmod{26}, c_2-k_2\boldmod{26},\dots c_n-k_n\boldmod{26}) \end{align*}
    for each key \(k=(k_1,k_2,\dots, k_n)\in \mathcal{K}\text{,}\) plaintext \(p=(p_1,p_2,\dots, p_n)\in \mathcal{P}\) and ciphertext \(c=(c_1,c_2,\dots, c_n)\in \mathcal{C}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In this setting a key \(k=(k_1,k_2,\dots, k_n)\) is called a keyword.
    🔗
    🔗

    The Vignère cipher was long believed to be uncrackable. Indeed it was dubbed the le chiffre indéchiffrable. However, once the the length of the keyword is determined, it is not difficult to determine the keyword itself using frequency analysis on substrings of the ciphertext. Furthermore, using the Kasiski method, we can often determine the length of the keyword by computing distances between repeated substrings (e.g., between repeated trigrams) in the ciphertext and looking for common factors of these distances. It likely that the keyword length is one of these common factors. Simon Singh has created a nice interactive demonstration of this method on his cryptography website The Black Chamber.     In 1922 William Friedman developed an alternative and more effective statistical tool for cracking Vigenere and other periodic substitution ciphers: the index of coincidence.
    🔗

    Definition 1.18.12. Index of coincidence.

    Let \(s\) be a string of length \(n\) consisting of letters from our alphabet. For all \(0\leq i\leq 25\text{,}\) let \(F_{i}\) be the number of times the \(i\)-th letter of the alphabet appears in \(S\text{.}\) We define the index of coincidence of \(s\) to be
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{IndCo}(s)=\sum_{i=0}^{25}\frac{F_{i}}{n}\frac{F_{i}-1}{n-1}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=0}^{25}F_{i}(F_{i}-1). \end{equation*}
    Similarly given two strings \(s\text{,}\) \(t\) of length \(n\) and \(m\text{,}\) respectively, and with respective counts \(F_{i}\) and \(F_{i}'\) for the \(i\)-th letter of the alphabet, we define the mutual index of coincidence of \(s\) and \(t\) to be
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{MutIndCo}(s,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{25}\frac{F_{i}}{n}\cdot\frac{F_{i}'}{m}=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=0}^{25}F_{i}F_{i}'. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    An elementary probabilistic argument shows that \(\operatorname{IndCo}(s)\) is the probability that two randomly selected letters from \(s\) are the same, and that \(\operatorname{MutIndCo}(s,t)\) is the probability that randomly selecting an element from \(s\) and \(t\) produces the same letter.
    🔗
    Suppose \(s\) and \(t\) are strings taken from natural English language. As \(n,m\rightarrow\infty\) the relative frequencies \(F_{i}/n\text{,}\) \(F'_{i}/m\) and \((F_{i}-1)/(n-1)\) all approach the natural relative frequency of the \(i\)-th letter of the alphabet, call it \(p_{i}\text{.}\) (Thus \(p_{0}=.082, p_{1}=.015,\dots, p_{25}=0.001)\text{.}\)) We see then as \(n,m\rightarrow\infty\) both \(\text{IndCo}(s)\) and \(\text{MutIndCo}(s,t)\) approach \(p_{0}^{2}+p_{1}^{2}+\cdots +p_{25}^{2}\approx 0.0656\text{.}\)
    🔗
    There is a clear connection here with linear algebra and inner products. Define the vector
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{p}=(p_{0},p_{1},\dots, p_{25})=(0.082,0.015,.028,\dots, 0.001,0.020,0.001) \end{equation*}
    Then using the dot product, we see that \(p_{0}^{2}+p_{1}^{2}+\cdots +p_{25}^{2}=\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{p}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now suppose \(s\) and \(t\) are random strings of characters. Then in this case as \(n,m\rightarrow\infty\) the various relative frequencies all approach \(1/26\text{,}\) and thus both \(\text{IndCo}(s)\) and \(\text{MutIndCo}(s,t)\) approach \(\frac{1}{26^{2}}+\frac{1}{26^{2}}+\cdots +\frac{1}{26^{2}}=\frac{1}{26}\approx 0.0385\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As in Case 1, we can define the vector \(\mathbf{r}=(\frac{1}{26},\dots, \frac{1}{26})\text{.}\) Then we see that for random sequences of characters the various indices of coincidence approach \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Continuing with this line of thought, suppose \(s\) is the result of taking some natural English and performing a simple substitution by \(\sigma\text{;}\) similarly suppose \(t\) is the result of taking some natural English and performing a simple substitution by \(\tau\text{.}\) Then as \(n,m\rightarrow\infty\) the various relative frequencies will still approach the natural relative frequencies, but with a reordering.
    🔗
    Now taking the vector \(\mathbf{p}\) from above, define \(\mathbf{p}^{\sigma}\) to be the same vector, but with entries permuted according to \(\sigma\text{.}\) Define \(\mathbf{p}^{\tau}\) similarly. Then as \(n,m\rightarrow\infty\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} \text{IndCo}(s),\text{IndCo}(t)\amp\rightarrow \mathbf{p}^{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{\sigma}\approx 0.0656 \\ \text{MutIndCo}(s,t) \amp \rightarrow \mathbf{p}^{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{\tau} \text{.} \end{align*}
    The last limit computation gives us a simple test to see whether \(s\) and \(t\) have used the same substitution permuation: when \(\sigma=\tau\text{,}\) we should have \(\text{MutIndCo}(s,t)\approx 0.0656\text{;}\) otherwise we should have \(\text{MutIndCo}(s,t)< 0.0656\text{.}\) The latter oberservation comes from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, which says that
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{p}^{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{\tau}\leq \vert\vert \mathbf{p}^{\sigma}\vert\vert \vert\vert\mathbf{p}^{\tau}\vert\vert. \end{equation*}
    If we restrict our attention to shift ciphers, then in this modern age we can just compute a \((26)\times(26)\) table of the various values \(\mathbf{p}^{s_i}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{s_j}\) (where \(s_{l}\) denotes the shift by \(l\) substitution) and compare our empirical result with these values. The following example illustrates how to use these indices of coincidence functions to decrypt a Vigenère cipher.

    Trying determining the keyword length using friedkeylength(c,k). The value \(k\) can be thought of as an upper bound on the potential keyword length. The function outputs, for each \(\ell\leq k\text{,}\) the indices of coincidence of each of the \(\ell\) substrings that the ciphertext is partioned into. You should be able to tell from those outputs which value of \(\ell\) is most likely to be the keyword length.
    🔗
    Once you have guess for the keyword length \(n\text{,}\) try running friedkeyword(c,n). Let \(k=(k_1,k_2,\dots, k_n)\) be the keyword (to be determined), and let \(s_1,s_2,\dots, s_n\) be the \(n\) substrings that the plaintext is partitioned into. The friedkeyword(c,k) function outputs a table, whose row labeled with \(i\) and \(j\) in the first two columns computes \(\operatorname{MutIndCo}(s_i, \sigma_\ell(s_j))\text{,}\) where \(\sigma_\ell\) is the shift by \(\ell\) substitution. From our discussion above, when this value is close to \(0.065\text{,}\) the strings \(s_i\) and \(\sigma_\ell(s_j)\) are both likely to be the shift of some natural English by the same shift value. Since \(s_i\) is the the result of shifting some natural English by \(k_i\text{,}\) and \(\sigma_\ell(s_j)\) is the result of shifting some natural English by \(k_j+\ell\text{,}\) we conclude that \(k_i\equiv k_j+\ell\pmod{26}\text{.}\) Proceeding in this way, and considering the \(k_i\) as unknowns, we are able to produce a linear system of equations in the unknowns \(k_i\) of the form
    \begin{align*} k_1-k_2 \amp \equiv \ell_1 \pmod{26}\\ k_2-k_3 \amp \equiv \ell_2 \pmod{26}\\ \vdots \amp \vdots\\ k_{n-1}-k_n \amp \equiv \ell_{n-1} \pmod{26}\\ k_n-k_1 \amp \equiv \ell_n \pmod{26}\text{,} \end{align*}
    where the \(\ell_i\) are the various “correct” shift values we have determined empirically. It can be shown that, modulo 26, every solution to the system above is of the form
    \begin{align} k_n\amp \equiv c \pmod{26} \tag{1.18.3}\\ k_{n-1} \amp \equiv \ell_{n-1}+c \pmod{26}\tag{1.18.4}\\ k_{n-2} \amp \equiv \ell_{n-1}+\ell_{n-2}+c \pmod{26}\tag{1.18.5}\\ \vdots \amp \vdots\tag{1.18.6}\\ k_1 \amp \equiv \ell_{n-1}+\ell_{n-2}+\cdots +\ell_1+c \pmod{26}\text{,}\tag{1.18.7} \end{align}
    where \(c\in \{0,1,\dots,25\}\text{.}\) To finish determining the keyword, we try each of these 26 different values of \(c\) until we find a value whose key \(k\) decrypts the ciphertext to something sensible. We leave you with a few empty Sage cells to try these methods out.
